Starlink Constellation Resilience & Space-Safety Summary

Snapshot 2026-10-17 · 10,652 public Starlink TLEs (CelesTrak) · Consolidated findings from analysis Parts 1–3

Scope. A defensive resilience and space-safety study built only on public data. It contains no targeting, no attack optimisation and no de-anonymisation. All results are aggregated by altitude shell or inclination group.

  1. Coverage is extremely robust. The fleet covers 100% of the globe at a 25° elevation mask, with about 45 satellites in view per ground point. Under random loss, coverage stays above 99% until about 85% of satellites are lost; it drops below 95% at about 92% loss and below 90% at about 94.5%.
  2. Correction: the earlier “altitude-band fragmentation” finding was an artifact. It measured the largest connected component (LCC) as a share of the original fleet instead of the survivors. Measured correctly with realistic link ranges (≥1,000 km), the surviving satellites stay one connected component, even after removing the most populated 10-km band (51% of the fleet). Real fragmentation appears only at a 500-km link range or with very local k-nearest-neighbour link models.
  3. The real risk is congestion in the 460–490 km shells. These three shells are 10–100× more congested than any other altitude and carry ~97% of the modelled close-encounter rate.
  4. No runaway Kessler cascade in the dense shells. Drag clears debris below 500 km within months (median fragment life 0.07–0.12 yr). One breakup yields fewer than one catastrophic secondary collision (≤≈0.32 with no avoidance, ≈0.016 with 95% avoidance). Debris at 580–590 km lasts about 10× longer (median 0.72 yr).
  5. The biggest redundancy gap is the polar (~97°) group (1,355 satellites). Without it, coverage poleward of 60° falls from 100% to 82.5%, and mean satellites in view there drop from 20 to 6.5.
  6. The main residual hazard is small, untrackable debris. 1–10 cm fragments cannot be avoided; the model gives about 10² mission-degrading hits per breakup (an upper bound).

Overall confidence: High on coverage robustness and the artifact correction. Medium on connectivity (link graphs are proxies for the real laser mesh). Low–medium on absolute collision and cascade numbers — the shell ranking is the robust result.

  • Reff (catastrophic secondaries per breakup) is below 1 in every band, so the model shows no runaway cascade from breakups within the fleet. The main reason is atmospheric drag below 500 km.
  • ~80% of fragments leave their parent 10-km shell; a breakup anywhere in 460–500 km exposes all three dense shells.
  • Space-safety ranking: 480–490 ≈ 470–480 > 460–470 ≫ 490–500 ≈ 580–590 > 540–560 ≫ 360–370.
  • 580–590 km holds few satellites, but its debris persists ~6–10× longer than in the dense shells and reaches more non-Starlink traffic (not counted here).
Threshold sensitivity. If every ≥10 cm fragment were counted as catastrophic, the no-avoidance Reff in the dense shells would be ~2.0–2.1 (above 1); with 95% avoidance it would be ~0.10. The “no runaway” conclusion therefore depends on the ≥30 cm lethality threshold and on active avoidance.

7. Corrections made during the analysis

  1. Altitude-band “fragmentation” withdrawn — denominator was the original fleet, not survivors (see §2).
  2. Catastrophic-fragment threshold set to ≥30 cm (~40 J/g on an 800-kg satellite at 8–10 km/s). 10–30 cm hits are reported as tracked/avoidable; 1–10 cm as untrackable/mission-degrading.
  3. Altitude binning reconciled. Parts 1–2 use single-instant altitudes (5,433 sats in 470–480 km); Part 3 uses orbit-averaged altitudes (460–470: 3,297; 470–480: 2,376; 480–490: 3,526). The 450–500 km totals agree (9,270 vs 9,273).
  4. Part 1 Kessler model demoted to an illustration, replaced by the Part 3 physics-based breakup model.
  5. Part 2 re-run on a lighter setup (20-min steps, 6 snapshots) after the full run ran out of memory; headline numbers match Part 1.

Limitations

  • Public TLEs only, propagated with SGP4 on a spherical Earth.
  • Coverage is a geometric 25° mask, not service: no capacity, beam or gateway model.
  • Connectivity uses proximity or k-NN proxies; it ignores Earth occlusion, pointing limits and routing.
  • Only Starlink-vs-Starlink debris exposure is modelled. Other operators, rocket bodies, the existing debris background and the 500–1,000 km debris belt are excluded — this is not a full Kessler assessment.
  • Lifetime model is a one-parameter exponential; fragment orbits stay fixed until decay (no gradual descent through lower shells).
  • Avoidance (95%) applies only to tracked objects (≥10 cm).
  • Parts 1–2 and Part 3 bin altitudes differently.

Appendix: Methods

ItemApproach
CatalogCelesTrak public GP data (Starlink group, active catalog Starlink entries, supplemental elements), deduplicated by NORAD ID → 10,652 satellites.
PropagationSGP4, spherical Earth (R = 6,371 km). Part 1: 15-min steps, 24 hourly connectivity snapshots. Part 2: 20-min steps, 6 snapshots. Part 3: orbit-averaged altitudes (12 samples over ~one orbit).
CoverageFibonacci ground grid (2,000 pts Parts 1–2; 1,500 pts × 4 snapshots Part 3). Covered if ≥1 satellite ≥25° above the horizon. Part 3 random-loss coverage computed exactly as 1 − fk.
ConnectivityProximity graph, ISL range 500–5,000 km (baseline 2,000 km); alternatives: shell + cross-shell links, k-NN (k = 4, 8). Metrics: LCC / survivors, # components, algebraic connectivity, inter-shell links.
Failure modesRandom loss; whole orbital planes (1° inclination × 5° RAAN bins); whole 10-km altitude bands, largest first.
CongestionKinetic-gas encounter rates per 10-km shell, latitude-resolved density, mean relative velocity, random phasing (upper bound). Hard-body radius 5 m; close-approach sphere 1 km.
Breakup / cascadeNASA Standard Breakup Model, one catastrophic breakup of an 800-kg satellite; isotropic Δv; per-shell residence time. Lifetime τ = 3 yr · (0.01 / (A/m)) · exp((hp − 475) / 55). Exposure counted against Starlink satellites only.
